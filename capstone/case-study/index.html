Project Overview

Seabnb is an interactive learning game that allows users to learn about floating cities. Floating cities and settlements have existed in various forms for hundreds of years, from the artificial islands of the Aztecs and the Uros, to micronations such as the Principality of Sealand. My project focuses on floating city projects currently being developed in the modern day, primarily as a response to climate change.

My target audience for this project is teenagers and young adults living in cities and communities at risk of being displaced by rising sea levels. I want them to be conscient of how climate change will have an effect on their futures, but also realize that there are ways we can adapt. I decided my project would resemble a text-based game to appeal to my audience and allow them to explore my topic in-depth.

My first main goal was to bring awareness to the topic of floating cities. I wanted to instill in my users the same sense of curosity that I had when first hearing and researching about this topic. I also want them to critically think about future solutions to their future problems, and not to be overly pessimistic or passive.

Project Research

When doing my research, I first did some preliminary research on the concept of floating cities, looking at some historical examples like the previously mentioned Uros and Aztec artificial islands. I also did some comparative analysis by examining games based around similar topics. I looked at Coastal Futures, a tabletop-style online game where the purpose was to try and secure your family's well-being in the wake of rising sea levels, and The Climate Game, a choice-based game where the user has to aim to bring the world to net-zero and save it from the worst effects of climate change. I was inspired by the flat graphic style of Coastal Futures and the game flow of The Climate Game.

However, it was around this time that the direction of my project shifted slightly. I decided to perform more research into modern-day attempts at making floating cities, primarily Maldives Floating City, Oceanix Busan, and Dogen City. Though currently still in-progress, these projects were explicitly conceptualized as a response to climate change, making them more relevant to the purpose of my project. I drew from my own family's experience with managing Airbnb properties, and designed the interactive experience around having the user learn about these floating cities and making judgements on which would be the best to live in.

Visual Design

Through my storyboards and paper sketches, I had a general sense of what types of visuals I wanted to use for each section of my project from the beginning. Similar to The Climate Game, I chose to have different illustrated backgrounds tailored to each section, though with a more flat graphic style. For the specific floating city sections, I used concept images of the cities as reference (though they are simplified signficantly in comparison). Translating my initial sketches to digital illustrations with Adobe Illustrator proved to be a difficult process for me, with production lasting until the final stages of the project. If I were to do the project over, I would've spent more time developing digital comps and determing 'how' I'd developed each graphic before moving on to the coding process.

In contrast, I determined my ideal typeface, Raleway, fairly early on. Raleway is a typeface that is fairly similar to Airbnb's official font, and could be flexibly used as both heading and body text. It's clean-looking and easy-to-read, while not being totally generic. To further reference Airbnb, I designed my own logo with similar dimensions and emphasis on curved lines, though this logo combined the letter 'S' with an ocean wave/water droplet.

User Interface

From the initial development, the user interaction was planned to be fairly simple, with users clicking through various lines of information and choosing different dialogue options to learn more, like a basic text-based adventure. I planned to have users "journey" to different floating cities through a basic image selection. It was Mahima who suggested I put this location selection on a map to emphasize a 'travelling' concept, and I believe it significantly enhanced the project's visual interest and informative value.

Especially in the earlier iterations of the project, I used orange buttons to indicate interactable elements progression. I chose orange because it is an attention grabbing color that stands out among the shades of blues that cover most of the website. Later on in development, I'd also use orange to highlight important keywords as well.

The basic flow of the project goes like this:

  1. On the landing page, the user clicks the "start" button to begin the interactive.
  2. The user goes through an onboarding process that explains the 'backstory' and purpose of Seabnb and the user's goal of travelling to different floating cities.
  3. The user is sent to a map view and is prompted to select a floating city location to learn more about.
  4. In each city, the user will receive a introduction on the floating city, and is given a selection of possible lines of query to pursue. The user can ask any questions they want, or none at all. Once the user selects "No further questions", they are sent back to the map view.
  5. Once the user is satisfied, they click "Done Exploring" and are prompted to choose the floating city they'd like to live in the most, leading to the ending message.

Revisions

Although I wasn't able to build the full scope of my project before usability testing started, I was able to gain valuable feedback on how to improve the interactions present on my page. Specifically, I was advised to remove unnecessary interaction points, such as the confirmation message that prompted if the user was sure they wanted to go to a specific location. This confirmation message often confused user testers momentarily and interrupted the flow of the project, so I eliminated it.

I did include a confirmation message before users moved on to the ending phase of the project, to discourage users from skipping to the end prematurely without outright forcing them to go through all of the content. I also made sure to work on the little details, like removing the 'click' indicator when the user is expected to select a dialogue option and graying out dialogue options once the user is finished with a line of query.

I ended completely overhauling the visual presentation of the project, mostly because I felt that the project in its previous state was too generic and undercooked to be satisfying to go through. I started with implementing particle js to create a bubble effect in the background to add some visual flair. I redesigned the textbox to be sleeker and allowed users to simply click on the textboxes to advance (as indicated by the flashing mouse icons, which replaced the "next" buttons in previous iterations of my design).

Summary

I was able to learn a lot about working on a large scale interactive project, such as how to implement specific Javascript functions and libraries, how to do in-depth research on a topic and present it in a cohesive manner, and how important each step of research and development is.

I put a lot of effort into making the visual design and interactions appealing, even going redoing entire steps to make sure the quality matched my personal standards and the 'high-tech' expectations users may have of these floating city projects.

Making text content and individual images for each floating city and making sure my code functioned properly and smoothly were grueling tasks, and there was a lot of content that was cut. Seeing my project as a cohesive whole, I'm satisfied with what I was able to achieve.
